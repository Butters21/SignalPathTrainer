EQ (equalisation) turns frequency ranges up or down. On a live desk it does three jobs: removing what you don't want (rumble, mud, harshness), helping each instrument find its own space in the mix, and controlling feedback. Good EQ is mostly small cuts. If a channel needs huge boosts, the problem is usually the mic, its position or the source.

Basics

The EQ shapes on every desk

Every channel strip has some mix of these filters. A parametric band lets you set three things: frequency (where), gain (how much up or down, in dB) and Q (how wide). A graphic EQ has fixed bands with one fader each. It's normally on outputs such as wedges and the main PA, not on channels.

Q: how wide is the band?

A low Q (0.5–1) is wide and gentle: it sounds musical and natural, so use it for tone shaping. A high Q (4–10) is narrow and surgical: use it to remove one ringing note, a feedback frequency or a resonance without changing the rest of the sound.

Octaves and frequency

Every time frequency doubles you go up one octave: 100 → 200 → 400 → 800 Hz. That's why EQ scales are logarithmic, with 20–200 Hz taking as much room as 2–20 kHz. Our hearing works the same way.

Habits

EQ habits on a live desk

  1. Gain first, then EQ. EQ on a channel with the wrong gain won't fix it.
  2. High-pass almost everything except kick, bass and sub-heavy keys. Every channel's rumble adds up into a muddy PA and takes amplifier power.
  3. Cut before you boost. Taking away what's wrong usually sounds more natural than adding, and boosts bring a channel closer to feedback.
  4. Small moves. 2–4 dB is a lot live. If you need 10 dB, change the mic or its position.
  1. Judge in the mix, not in solo. A guitar that sounds thin on its own may sit perfectly with the band.
  2. Sweep to find problems: boost a narrow band, sweep until the problem jumps out, then turn the boost into a cut. Keep the level down near wedges, because a boost can start feedback.
  3. Channel EQ shapes tone; output EQ fixes the room and feedback. The graphic EQ on the PA or a wedge affects everything going through it.
  4. Check at show level. Ears hear less bass and treble at low volume, so EQ set quietly sounds different when the show is loud.

Dynamics processors react to level. A compressor turns loud moments down so a vocal or bass sits at a steadier level. A gate turns a channel down when it goes quiet, cutting the bleed between drum hits. Used gently, they make a mix easier to control. Used heavily, they suck the life out of it and bring feedback closer.

Basics

What a compressor does

  • Threshold: the level where the compressor starts working. Anything quieter passes untouched.
  • Ratio: how hard it pushes back. At 4:1, a note 8 dB over the threshold comes out only 2 dB over.
  • Attack: how quickly it reacts. Slow attack lets the punch of a drum or pluck through; fast attack catches it.
  • Release: how quickly it lets go. Too fast sounds pumpy; too slow keeps squashing the next note.
  • Knee: soft knee eases in around the threshold; hard knee kicks in exactly at it.
  • Make-up gain: turns the whole signal back up after compression.

The gain reduction (GR) meter

This is the meter to watch. It shows how many dB the compressor is turning down right now. For most live channels, 3–6 dB on the loudest notes is plenty. If it never moves, the compressor isn't doing anything; if it never comes back to zero, it's squashing everything.

Why it matters live

Compression raises the quiet parts relative to the loud ones, and that includes the stage spill and wedge sound the mic picks up. Heavy compression on a vocal mic in front of a wedge brings feedback closer, so compress less live than you would in a studio.

Gates

Gates and expanders

A gate is a door: above the threshold it opens; below it, it closes and turns the channel down by the range. On toms it removes the kick, snare and cymbal bleed between hits, which tightens the kit and stops toms ringing on. Set attack fast so the hit isn't chopped, hold long enough for the drum's sustain, and release so the tail fades naturally. An expander is a gentler gate: it turns quiet signal down a little instead of shutting it off.

Try: threshold too low and the bleed opens it; too high and quiet tom hits are missed. A very short release chops the tom's ring and sounds unnatural. Don't gate vocals or anything that plays softly. If you must, use an expander with a small range.

More tools

De-essers, limiters and order

De-esser

A compressor that only listens to the 5–8 kHz area, so it turns down harsh "s" and "t" sounds without dulling the whole vocal. Set the frequency by sweeping until the sibilance triggers it, aiming for a few dB of GR on the worst ones.

Limiter

A compressor with a very high ratio (∞:1) and fast attack. It stops anything going over a set level. Use it on outputs to protect speakers and in-ear mixes, not to make a mix louder.

Multiband and dynamic EQ

These compress only one frequency range at a time, for example taming boom on a vocal only when the singer gets close to the mic.

Mixing a band live means making every part clear and the most important things easy to hear, in a room full of people, without feedback, and without a second take. This page brings gain, EQ, dynamics and routing together: how to build a mix, why instruments fight, how to pan for a whole room, and how to use reverb and delay.

Method

How to build a live mix

  1. Gain and high-pass first on every channel during line check, so every fader works in the same range.
  2. Decide what matters most. In nearly every show it's the lead vocal. Everything else is mixed around it.
  3. Build the foundation: kick and bass together, then snare, then the rest of the kit quietly underneath.
  4. Add the vocal to sit clearly on top. If you can't understand the words, the mix isn't working.
  1. Fit the instruments in around the vocal: guitars, keys, then extras. If two things fight, change one with EQ, panning or level.
  2. Add effects last and keep them under the dry sound.
  3. Walk the room, or at least listen from the middle, at the back and near the front edge.
  4. Keep mixing. A live mix is performed: ride the vocal, push the solos, pull things back between songs.

Masking: why instruments fight

When two instruments put energy in the same frequency range at the same time, the louder one hides the quieter one. This is masking. Electric guitar, keys and the lead vocal all crowd the 200 Hz–4 kHz area, and kick and bass share the bottom end. Turning the vocal up only works until it feeds back. Making room works better.

Ways to make room

  • High-pass everything that doesn't need deep lows, so the bass and kick have the bottom to themselves.
  • Cut where another part needs to be heard: a few dB around 2–4 kHz on guitars and keys lets the vocal's words through.
  • Give kick and bass separate jobs: one owns the deep thump, the other the note. A small cut in each at the other's strongest frequency helps.
  • Pan similar instruments apart, gently.
  • Arrangement first: if two parts play the same notes in the same octave, ask the band. No EQ fixes that completely.

Hear it

On the mixer, set the challenge "The vocal is buried", then turn Make room on and off without touching the faders. The vocal gets clearer even though nothing got louder.

Panning for a whole room

At home you sit in the middle of two speakers. At a gig most of the audience doesn't: people on the left mostly hear the left speaker. Pan a guitar hard right and the left side of the room loses it. Move the slider and see how loud the guitar is in each seat.

  • within 2 dB
  • 2–6 dB down

Live panning rules of thumb

  • Keep lead vocal, kick, snare and bass in the centre: they're the core of the mix and everyone must hear them.
  • Pan support instruments a little (about 20–40%) for width, not hard left and right.
  • Many venues run the PA in mono, or have fills and delays that are mono. Then panning does nothing, so the mix has to work without it.
  • Stereo keys and effect returns are fine wide, because the important information is in both sides.
Effects

Reverb and delay in a live mix

Reverb

Reverb makes a sound seem to be in a space. On a desk it usually lives on an FX return fed by aux sends, so many channels share one reverb and you control how much each gets.

  • Plate: bright and smooth, the classic vocal and snare reverb.
  • Hall: long and big, good for ballads and orchestral work; it can turn to mush in a lively room.
  • Room: short, makes drums sound natural.
  • Pre-delay (20–80 ms) separates the dry voice from the reverb, keeping words clear.
  • The room already adds reverb. Use less in a reverberant hall and more in a dead club or outdoors.

Delay

Delay repeats the sound. Slapback (80–150 ms, one repeat) thickens rock and country vocals. Tempo delays are timed to the song so the repeats fall on the beat: a quarter note at 120 BPM is 500 ms. Tap the tempo in time with the drummer.

  • Feedback sets how many repeats; keep it low so repeats don't build up.
  • A high cut in the delay return keeps repeats behind the dry voice.
  • Mute effects between songs so the singer's talking isn't drenched in reverb, and push the delay into the last word of a line.

On the mixer, raise the vocal's Delay send, then press Tap tempo on the beat a few times.

Loud sound and mains power are the two parts of a live-sound job that can injure you or someone else. This page covers how much sound is safe and for how long, how to protect your ears and the audience's, and the power basics to know before you plug anything in. Always follow your venue's rules and your country's regulations: the numbers here are a guide, not a legal reference.

Hearing

How loud, for how long

Hearing damage depends on two things together: how loud and how long. Sound meters for hearing use the A-weighting (dBA), which follows how sensitive our ears are at each frequency. Every safety rule sets a level you can take for a full 8-hour day, then an exchange rate: each time the level goes up by that many dB, the safe time halves.

Hearing

Protecting ears at work

Your ears

  • Wear plugs from line check onwards, not just during the show. Filtered musician's plugs cut level evenly, so the mix still sounds right, only quieter.
  • Take quiet breaks. Your ears recover better with time in a quiet place between loud sessions.
  • Ringing ears or a dull, muffled feeling after a show is a warning that you've had too much. If it lasts more than a day, see a doctor.
  • In-ear monitors can be safer than wedges because they block the stage, but only if the level is kept sensible. Use a limiter on IEM mixes.
  • Get a hearing test every year or two so you have a record of your own baseline.

The audience's ears

  • Measure, don't guess. Keep an SPL meter or measurement app at FOH logging LAeq (average) and peak levels.
  • The WHO's safe-listening standard for venues suggests an average of no more than 100 dB LAeq over any 15 minutes for the audience, with free earplugs and quiet areas available.
  • Many venues and councils set their own limits, including for noise outside the building. Find out at the advance.
  • Keep the front row in mind: it's often much louder than FOH. Front fills and sub placement change that.
  • Low frequencies feel loud but read low on dBA. Many shows also track dBC for bass.
Power

Mains power basics

Audio gear runs on the same mains power as everything else in the building, and mistakes with it can kill. A sound tech isn't usually the electrician, but you need to recognise what's safe, what isn't, and when to stop and call someone qualified.

Before you plug in

  • Check every cable and plug for cuts, crushed sections, loose strain reliefs or scorch marks. Damaged items get taped with a fault label and taken out of use.
  • Never remove or defeat the safety earth (ground) on a mains plug or adapter to cure hum. Fix hum on the audio side instead (see below).
  • Use outlets protected by an RCD (UK/EU) or GFCI (US) where you can, especially outdoors or near drinks and water.
  • Put audio on its own circuits, away from lighting dimmers and heavy motors, which cause buzz and spikes.
  • Many countries require regular electrical testing of portable equipment (PAT testing in the UK). Check the labels.

On the job

  • Power up in order: desk and processing first, amps and powered speakers last. Power down in the reverse order. This avoids loud thumps that can damage speakers.
  • Don't overload or daisy-chain extension blocks. Uncoil cable drums fully under load, because a coiled drum can overheat.
  • Generators and three-phase supplies must be connected by a qualified person. Your job is to tell them your load and spread it evenly across the phases.
  • Run cables where people won't trip on them, use cable ramps across walkways, and keep fire exits clear.
  • If you get a tingle from a mic or guitar, or something smells hot, stop and disconnect. Don't keep playing to finish the song.
Power

Load calculator

Add up what's on one circuit and see how hard you're working it. Current (amps) = power (watts) ÷ voltage. For a long show, keep the steady load under about 80% of the breaker rating. Use the average power from the spec sheet, not the peak figure, and allow headroom for loud passages.

The gain structure routine

  1. Faders at 0 (unity), EQ and dynamics flat or bypassed.
  2. Ask for the loudest part: the chorus, the hardest snare hit, the shouted line.
  3. PFL/solo the channel and raise gain until peaks reach about −12 to −6 dBFS. The average sits near −18.
  4. Watch it through soundcheck. Back off if the peak LED ever lights.
  5. Now add HPF, EQ and dynamics. Adjust compressor make-up so the post-dynamics level matches pre-dynamics.
  6. Mix with faders. If a fader ends up far from unity, revisit gain before the show, not during it.

Why it matters

  • Too much gain clips the A/D (analogue-to-digital) converter. That distortion is baked in; no fader or EQ later can remove it.
  • Too little gain leaves the signal near the noise floor. Pushing the fader to compensate raises the hiss with it, and leaves you no fader travel.
  • Gain also feeds monitors. Pre-fader aux sends follow the preamp. Changing gain mid-show changes every wedge and IEM mix that channel is in.
  • On shared-stagebox systems (FOH and monitors on one preamp) agree who owns gain, or use digital trim on one side.
Mix bus

Summing: why the master fader can't save you

Every channel you add to a mix raises the level on the bus. Two channels at the same level add about 3 dB if they're unrelated sounds, and 6 dB if they're identical. Eight similar channels add about 9 dB.

The fix

  • Pull the channel faders (or sends) feeding that bus down together, so the bus stops clipping. A DCA over the whole band is quick for this.
  • Then raise the master or the system level if you need more volume.
  • Keep master faders near unity. If the master sits at −15, the channels are too hot.

Pan law

  • A pan pot sends a channel to left and right. In the centre, each side is about 3 dB lower than panned hard to one side, so the total power stays roughly the same as you pan.
  • On a mono PA, everything ends up centre-summed anyway: check that hard-panned parts don't disappear for half the audience.

Aux (mix) buses

Each channel has a send level to every aux. The aux master goes to an output.

  • Pre-fader for monitors: the performer's mix doesn't change when you move the FOH fader.
  • Post-fader for effects: when you pull a vocal down, its reverb follows it.
  • Put graphic or parametric EQ on the aux output to ring out wedges.

Groups and DCAs

Both give you "one fader for the drums". They work differently.

  • Subgroup: audio is summed onto a bus. You can compress or EQ the whole kit. It adds a summing stage and needs routing (channels off LR, group to LR) or you get doubled signal.
  • DCA: no audio passes through it. It moves the channel faders remotely, so post-fader sends follow it too. Nothing to mis-route.

Mix bus (LR)

The main stereo or LCR sum. Everything you want the audience to hear ends up here.

  • Mix-bus processing (gentle compression, system EQ, limiter) sits on its insert.
  • Keep master near unity. Headroom is managed on the channel faders, not by burying the master.
  • On a floating-point mix engine the internal bus rarely clips, but the output converter and the system processor still will.

Matrix

A mixer of buses. Inputs are buses (LR, groups, auxes), outputs feed different parts of the PA.

  • Front fill gets LR at −6 dB with extra vocal group for intelligibility.
  • Delay towers get LR with output delay (about 2.9 ms per metre of distance) so they arrive in time with the mains.
  • Record/stream gets LR plus audience mics, independent of room level.
  • If one zone is silent and the rest work, check the matrix send and matrix master first.

Inserts, sends and plugin servers

Three ways a processor joins the signal. An insert takes the whole signal out and back; a send takes a copy; a plugin server does the processing on another computer over the network.

Native or DSP server?

  • Native plugin hosts run on the computer's own processor. Plugin count and latency depend on that computer and its audio driver buffer.
  • DSP servers (Waves SoundGrid is the common example) do the work on dedicated hardware: lower, steadier latency, more plugins, and the option of a backup server.
  • The desk connects through an I/O card or interface, usually on its own dedicated network and switch.

Show habits

  • Desk snapshots may also recall the plugin settings. If a plugin should stay put between scenes, set it recall-safe.
  • Keep an eye on the server's processing and memory load; reboot between shows, not during one.
  • Know your bypass: if the server or host computer fails, which channels go silent, and how do you get them back?
  • Mixing a plugin-processed path with a dry copy of the same source needs the delays to match, or it goes phasey.

Processing on the mix bus

Bus compression

  • Gentle glue, not control: around 1.5:1 to 2:1, slow attack, auto or medium release.
  • Aim for 1–3 dB of gain reduction on the loudest parts. More than that and the whole mix pumps.
  • Many engineers leave live LR compression off, and control dynamics on channels and groups instead.

Limiting and EQ

  • A limiter belongs on the outputs (often in the system processor) as protection, not as a way to get louder.
  • System EQ (room tuning) belongs in the processor or on the matrix outputs, not on the LR bus, so it isn't recalled away with a scene.
  • Keep the mix bus for the mix; keep the system for the room.

    The microphone is the start of the signal and the biggest single factor in how a source sounds and how loud it can get before feedback. Choosing the right type, pattern and position does more than any amount of EQ.

    Handbook: Sec. 10, 5.3, 11.11
    Types

    Three ways to turn sound into signal

    Dynamic

    Sound moves a diaphragm with a coil attached, and the coil moving in a magnet's field generates the signal. No power needed.

    • Rugged, cheap, handles very loud sources
    • Lower sensitivity: needs more preamp gain
    • Most live vocals, snare, toms, guitar amps, kick

    Condenser

    A very thin diaphragm and a fixed plate form a capacitor. Sound changes the gap, and a small built-in amplifier turns that into signal. Needs phantom power (or a battery).

    • Detailed, sensitive, extended highs
    • Picks up more of the room and the wedges
    • Overheads, acoustic guitar, choirs, hi-hat, some vocals

    Ribbon

    A thin strip of metal between magnet poles moves with the sound. Smooth and natural, with a figure-8 pattern.

    • Warm, smooth top end
    • Fragile: wind, drops and faulty phantom cabling can damage older ones
    • Mostly studio; live on guitar amps and brass

    Theatre

    Theatre and body mics

    In theatre the mic goes on the performer, not in front of them. Pick a position to compare how close it gets to the mouth, and what that costs and buys you.

    Why omni lavs are normal in theatre

    • The mic is so close to the mouth that its pattern matters less than its distance. Close and omni usually beats further and directional.
    • An omni sounds the same when the actor turns their head, sits in a wig, or hides under a collar: off-axis tone barely changes.
    • Omnis don't have proximity effect, so a chest mic doesn't go boomy, and they're less sensitive to wind and breath.
    • Some lavs take swappable caps that brighten or soften the top end for hair or chest positions.

    Fixing it to the actor

    • Plan placements with wigs and wardrobe before the first tech: hats, glasses, quick changes and stage combat all change the answer.
    • Use medical tape or skin adhesive on skin, never gaffer tape. Clean the skin first so it holds through sweat.
    • Leave a slack loop where the neck turns: have the actor look hard left and right while you tape.
    • Inside costumes, tape or sew the cable to the fabric so it can't rub. Synthetic fabrics are the noisiest.
    • Label every mic and pack with the actor's name.

    Sweat, make-up and spares

    • A capsule soaked with sweat can go dull, crackle or stop working ("sweat-out"). Often it recovers once dried; a drying box with silica gel helps overnight.
    • Keep make-up and hairspray out of the grille; use a cover and replace a clogged grille rather than washing it.
    • A drip loop and a sweat ring on the cable stop sweat running down into the capsule or the connector.
    • Leads and actors who sweat a lot are often double-miked: two mics and two packs, so a failure is a fader move, not a show stop.
    • Check every mic before every show: plug in, listen, wiggle the cable and connector.

    Area mics: boundary and overhead

    • Boundary mics lie on the stage near the front edge, aimed back at the action. Cheap coverage, but they hear footsteps and scenery.
    • Overhead (choir) mics hang above the stage. Aim for a little in front of the nearest actors and not far above head height, angled slightly upstage, because actors project out, not up.
    • Use as few area mics as you can: every extra open mic costs gain before feedback.
    • Space them by the 3:1 rule to avoid comb filtering. They will never match a mic on each performer.

    Gain before feedback

    The handbook's potential acoustic gain (PAG) equation: how much louder the system can make a talker for the furthest listener before it feeds back. Each distance doubling is worth 6 dB, and each doubling of open mics costs 3 dB.

    On a monitor wedge the listener is the performer, so the wedge (D2) is close, which helps, and the mic is close to the wedge (D1), which hurts. What saves you is the mic's rejection at the wedge (up to about 15–20 dB for a cardioid aimed well) and a close talker. That's why and keeping singers on the mic matter so much.

    Wireless

    Wireless microphones

    How it works

    • A handheld has the transmitter in the handle; a bodypack takes a lavalier, headset or instrument cable.
    • The receiver turns the radio signal back into audio and feeds the desk at mic or line level.
    • Analogue systems use a compander (compress before transmission, expand after) to reduce hiss. You may hear it as slight breathing on quiet passages.
    • Digital systems avoid companding, but add a little latency.

    Dropouts and diversity

    • Radio reflects off walls, trusses and people. Reflections arriving out of step can cancel at the antenna: a multipath dropout.
    • Diversity receivers use two antennas spaced apart (at least half a wavelength) and pick or combine the stronger signal at each moment.
    • Keep line of sight between transmitter and antennas. Mount antennas above head height, not behind racks or flight cases.

    Show-day checklist

    1. Scan and coordinate frequencies with the receivers' scan or coordination software, including other users nearby (TV, other stages, other departments).
    2. Avoid intermodulation: several transmitters create interference products; use coordinated frequency groups, not random picks.
    3. Fresh batteries (or charged packs) for every show. Note the time they went in.
    4. Walk the stage with every transmitter while watching RF and audio meters, especially the far corners.
    5. Set gain on the transmitter so loud singing doesn't overload it, then set desk gain as normal.
    6. Label every pack, mic and receiver channel to match.

    Wireless frequency rules have changed a lot since the handbook was written (Sec. 10.6, 1989). In the US, much of the old UHF range was reallocated; check your country's current rules and your equipment's legal bands before every tour.

    Three rules for frequencies

    1. Clean: each system on a frequency nothing else (TV, other stages, other departments) is using.
    2. Spaced: far enough from the next system. The minimum gap depends on the make; check the manual.
    3. Coordinated: clear of the "ghost" intermodulation products that two or more transmitters create together. Use the maker's frequency groups or coordination software.

    Digital systems usually fit more channels into the same space and are less prone to intermodulation, but the rules still apply.

    Packs and antennas

    • Never coil or stuff a pack's antenna into a pocket; keep it straight and clear of skin, sweat and metal.
    • Metallic fabric, armour and foil-backed costumes block radio. So, partly, does the body: move the pack or the antennas.
    • Receiver antennas need line of sight to the stage, ideally several metres from the performers, and short, low-loss cable.
    • Many receivers share a pair of antennas through an antenna distribution unit, which also keeps the rack tidy.
    • Don't leave lots of powered-up packs together on a table: they intermodulate. Lock pack menus so actors can't change settings.

    Setting it up

    • Set the gain on the pack for each performer's loudest singing, using the receiver's audio meter, then set the desk gain as usual.
    • Squelch mutes the receiver when the radio signal gets too weak to be clean. Too high and you get dropouts at range; too low and you hear hiss.
    • Tell the pack what battery type it's using so the battery meter reads correctly.
    • A backstage listening station lets the A2 hear every radio mic before it walks on.

    The ringing-out routine

    1. Set the stage exactly as it will be: mics on stands, wedges placed, performers' positions marked.
    2. Work on one monitor mix at a time, with the mic that matters most for that mix open.
    3. Talk into the mic and slowly raise the send until you hear a slight ring, not a full howl.
    4. Identify the frequency by ear or with an analyser. Pull that band down about 3 dB on the mix's output EQ.
    5. Raise the level again. Same frequency: cut a little more. New frequency: cut that one 3 dB.
    6. Stop when several frequencies ring at once. That's all the gain there is. Expect roughly 3–15 dB of extra gain from a good ring-out.
    7. Back off about 10 dB from that point for the show. Performances are louder than soundcheck.
    8. Repeat every day, even on the same stage. Temperature, humidity and layout all shift the resonances.

    Before you touch the EQ

    • Mic choice and aim do the most. Point the mic's dead spot at the wedge. Try the pattern tool on the tab.
    • Hand on the mic grille ("cupping") ruins the pattern. It turns a cardioid nearly omnidirectional and it will feed back. Ask performers, tactfully, not to do it.
    • Put the EQ on the mix output, not the channel. A channel cut changes the mic for every mix and for FOH.
    • High-pass the monitor mixes at 60–100 Hz, except for the drummer and anyone who needs low keys or bass.
    • Try flipping the wedge's polarity. It sometimes buys a little more level.
    • Two wedges for one performer: place them with rear corners touching and splay them apart. Wedges aimed so their coverage crosses cause comb filtering and feed back sooner.

    The words you'll hear

    • Input list: the plan. Channel number, source, mic or DI, stand, phantom, and stagebox socket for every input.
    • Stage plot: a drawing of where everyone and everything sits on stage.
    • Socket / input: a physical connector on the stagebox or desk.
    • Channel: a strip on the desk. On a digital desk, any socket can be patched to any channel.
    • Sub-snake / drop box: a small box on stage (e.g. by the drums) that feeds a block of stagebox inputs.
    • Tie line: a fixed cable between two places in a venue, e.g. stage to the FOH position.
    • Split: one source sent to two or more desks, such as FOH, monitors and a recording.

    Splitting and sharing preamps

    • Transformer (passive) split: one side gets a direct feed and supplies phantom; the other sides are isolated by transformers, which also stop ground loops between desks.
    • Y-split (wired in parallel): cheap, but both desks see each other's phantom and loading, and it can cause hum. Fine in a pinch, not for a show.
    • Digital split: one stagebox on a network, read by several desks. Only one desk controls the analogue gain and phantom.
    • Gain sharing: if monitors own the gain, FOH uses digital trim. Many systems offer gain compensation, so when the gain changes, the other desk's level stays put.
    • Agree at the start of the day who owns gain and phantom for each stagebox.

    Networked patching

    On a networked system the cable from the mic still goes into a physical socket, but after that the signal becomes a numbered channel on a computer network. Any desk or recorder on the network can then pick it up. Step through one vocal from the socket to a desk channel.

    Network patching words

    • Transmitter / receiver: a device sending channels onto the network, and one picking them up. Most devices are both.
    • Subscription (Dante) or stream / connection (AVB, AES67): telling a receiver channel which transmitter channel to listen to. This is the network's patch.
    • Digital split: several receivers subscribed to the same transmitter channel. No extra cable, no loss.
    • Gain owner: only one device controls the stagebox preamp. Everyone else uses digital trim.

    • Use the switches the system's maker recommends: gigabit, managed, with the audio quality-of-service settings applied.

    • Name every device and channel before the show; "Stagebox 05" beats "Ch 5 (2)".
    • Save the routing preset and print the patch, so a replacement device can be set up quickly.

    The chain after the desk

    Amp front-panel lights

    • Signal: audio is arriving. No signal LED = the problem is upstream.
    • Clip: the amp is running out of voltage. Occasional flicker on peaks is the limit; steady clip is too loud.
    • Limit: the limiter is working. Constantly on means you're asking more than the system can give.

    • Check polarity of every speaker with a polarity tester. One reversed box cancels its neighbour.

    Delay for fills and delay towers

    Sound travels about 343 m/s at 20 °C (roughly 1 ft per ms). A fill speaker further out must wait for the sound from the mains, or the audience hears an echo.

    Delay to set on the fill

    Measure from the mains to the fill along the path the sound travels to the listener. A few ms of extra delay uses the precedence (Haas) effect: listeners localise to whichever arrives first. Fine-tune by ear or with a measurement system.

    How loud at the back? (inverse square law)

    Outdoors, from a single point source, level drops 6 dB every time the distance doubles. Indoors, reflections stop the drop beyond a certain distance (the critical distance), and line arrays fall off more slowly in their near field. Use this for a rough estimate.

    Acoustics

    Rooms and acoustics

    The room is part of the sound system. Its size and surfaces decide how long sound hangs around, which bass notes boom, and how far from a speaker you can still understand speech.

    Absorb or isolate?

    • Absorption soaks up reflections inside the room: foam, fibreglass, curtains, carpet, people. Rated by NRC (0 to about 1).
    • Isolation stops sound getting through walls to the next room: mass, sealed gaps and decoupled layers. Rated by STC or transmission loss in dB.
    • They're different jobs: foam on a wall makes the room drier but does almost nothing for the neighbours.

    Reverberation and speech

    • Reverberation time (RT60) is how long a sound takes to fall by 60 dB after it stops.
    • Bigger rooms and harder surfaces ring longer. Speech wants less; orchestral music wants more.
    • Long reverb smears consonants. Beyond the critical distance from a speaker, the room's reverb is louder than the speaker's direct sound and intelligibility falls.
    • More speakers aimed well, closer to listeners, usually beats one loud system in a reverberant hall.

    Why balanced lines reject noise

    A balanced cable carries the signal twice: normal on the hot wire and upside down on the cold wire. Noise picked up along the cable lands on both wires the same way. The input subtracts cold from hot, which doubles the signal and cancels the noise.

    Soldering it

    1. Parts on first. Slide the boot, strain relief and shell (and any heatshrink) onto the cable before you solder. Everyone forgets this once.

    Networking basics

    • IP address: each device's address, e.g. 192.168.10.20. No two devices on a network can share one.
    • Subnet mask: says which part of the address is the network. With 255.255.255.0 (/24), devices must match in the first three numbers to talk directly.
    • Port: like an apartment number at that address. Each program listens on its own port.
    • UDP vs TCP: UDP fires a message and forgets it (fast, common for OSC). TCP sets up a connection and confirms delivery.
    • Static vs DHCP: show devices should use fixed (static) addresses or DHCP reservations. Otherwise an address can change after a reboot and every button pointing at it breaks.
    • Switch: connects the devices. Managed switches can split one switch into separate VLANs, e.g. control and audio.
    • Ping: the first test. If you can't ping it, nothing else will work.
    • For the full grounding, work through the Network course tab.

    MIDI

    Musical Instrument Digital Interface, from 1983 and still everywhere in show control. A 5-pin DIN cable carries one direction only, OUT → IN, at 31.25 kbit/s. USB MIDI and network MIDI carry the same messages.

    • 16 channels per cable. Sender and receiver must be on the same one.
    • Note On/Off: often used as simple triggers.
    • Program Change: commonly recalls a scene or preset.
    • Control Change (CC): continuous values 0–127, e.g. a fader.
    • MIDI Show Control (MSC): a standard set of commands (GO, STOP, RESUME, with a cue number) for lighting, sound and other show departments.
    • MIDI Timecode (MTC): timecode carried over MIDI.

    The classic trap: MIDI numbers start at 0 but many desks label scenes from 1. Program Change 5 may recall scene 6. Always test the offset.

    OSC (Open Sound Control)

    A network message with a readable address and optional values, usually sent over UDP. Much more flexible than MIDI, and the main way QLab, Companion and many desks talk to each other.

    /go → QLab: GO on the selected cue /cue/12/start → QLab: start cue 12 /ch/01/mix/fader 0.75 → X32: set ch 1 fader /ch/01/mix/on 0 → X32: mute ch 1 (on = 0)

    To send OSC you need three things right: the receiver's IP address, its port, and the exact address path. QLab listens on port 53000; the X32/M32 on 10023 (the X-Air series uses 10024).

    QLab as the show controller

    • Plays sound and video cues, but can also send Network cues (OSC to other devices), MIDI cues (Program Change, MSC to desks and lighting) and generate timecode.
    • It can be triggered by OSC, MIDI, MSC or incoming timecode.
    • OSC access can be protected by passcodes in the workspace's network settings. A sender without the right passcode is ignored.
    • Use a separate audio output for timecode and never route it into the PA mix.

    Companion and Stream Deck

    • Bitfocus Companion is free software running on a Mac, PC, Linux box or Raspberry Pi. It turns a Stream Deck (or a web page, tablet or other surface) into a programmable control panel.
    • You add a connection (module) for each device: QLab, X32, ATEM, a lighting desk, a projector. Each connection needs the device's IP address and the right port.
    • Each button has actions (what it sends) and feedbacks (what lights up, e.g. green when a channel is unmuted).
    • You set it up in its web interface on the Companion computer.
    • A red or "disconnected" status on a connection almost always means the IP, port, or network is wrong.

    SMPTE timecode

    A running clock written as HH:MM:SS:FF (hours, minutes, seconds, frames). It lets several systems lock to the same moment: lighting, video, pyro and sound all hit cue 47 at 01:03:12:08.

    • Frame rates: 24 (film), 25 (PAL/Europe), 29.97 (NTSC video, drop or non-drop), 30. Every device in the chain must agree.
    • Drop frame (29.97 DF): skips frame numbers 00 and 01 every minute except every tenth, so the label stays in step with the real clock. Non-drop at 29.97 runs 3.6 s per hour behind.
    • LTC (linear timecode) is sent as an audio signal. It sounds like a harsh buzz. MTC sends the same data over MIDI.
    • Chase: the receiver follows incoming timecode. Freewheel: it keeps counting for a short time if the signal drops out.
    • Start shows at 01:00:00:00, not zero. Rolling back from zero wraps to 23:59:59, which confuses receivers.
    • Send LTC at a steady level on a dedicated output, with no EQ, compression or gate on it.

    The method: follow the signal

    Don't guess. Start at the source and check each point in order until the signal stops. The fault is between the last place you had signal and the first place you don't.

    1. On stage. Is the source making sound? Mic on stand, cable plugged both ends, instrument volume up, DI battery/phantom, correct stagebox channel.
    2. Input meter. Is there level on the channel? No meter = problem before the desk: cable, patch, phantom, stagebox, network.
    3. PFL / solo. Listen in headphones. Meter moving but sounds wrong = processing (gate closed, HPF, polarity, insert).
    4. Channel out. Mute off, fader up, routed to LR or the right aux, DCA/mute group not pulling it down.
    5. Bus meter. Is it on the LR or aux meter? Check bus master, bus mute, bus insert.
    6. Matrix / output. Matrix send and master, output patch, correct physical output.
    7. Past the desk. Cable, processor, amp (power, protect LEDs, limiter), speaker.

    Two shortcuts: swap with a known-good part (cable, mic, channel) to find out which side the fault is on; and when the chain is long, check the middle first and halve the search each time.

    Addresses

    IP addresses and subnets

    Every device on a network needs an IP address, like 192.168.1.20. The subnet mask (or prefix, like /24) says how much of the address names the network and how much names the device. Devices can only talk directly to others on the same subnet; anything else needs a router, which most show networks don't have.

    • /24 (255.255.255.0) is the usual show subnet: 254 usable addresses.
    • Two devices with the same address both stop working properly. Keep an IP plan.
    • Private ranges for closed networks: 10.x.x.x, 172.16–31.x.x, 192.168.x.x.
    • 169.254.x.x means the device found no DHCP server and gave itself an address.

    Switches

    Switches, topology and loops

    • Build a star: a core switch at FOH or in the rack, with each area (stage left, stage right, amp racks, lighting) on its own branch. It's easy to find faults, and one failure only takes out one branch.
    • Daisy-chaining switches works for small rigs, but every hop adds latency and a single point of failure.
    • Never make a loop (two cables between the same switches, or a ring) unless every switch runs a loop-protection protocol such as Rapid Spanning Tree. An accidental loop floods the network and takes everything down within seconds.
    • Managed switches let you set QoS, VLANs, IGMP and EEE. Unmanaged switches are fine for a tiny, single-purpose network, as long as they don't use EEE.

    Cables and distance

    • Copper Ethernet (Cat5e, Cat6) reaches 100 m per run, including patch leads. Further needs a switch in between, or fibre.
    • Use etherCON or ruggedised connectors and shielded tactical cable for touring; plain office patch leads break.
    • Multimode fibre covers hundreds of metres; single-mode covers kilometres. The SFP modules at each end must match the fibre type and each other.
    • Label both ends of every run with what it is and where it goes.
    Switches

    VLANs: several networks on one switch

    A VLAN splits one physical switch into separate virtual networks. Audio, lighting, video and control can share the same hardware and fibre runs without seeing each other's traffic.

    • An access (untagged) port belongs to one VLAN: plug a device in and it's on that network.
    • A trunk (tagged) port carries several VLANs between switches, each packet labelled with its VLAN number.
    • Keep routing between VLANs off on show networks, and make sure clock and multicast don't leak across.

    Switches

    Multicast and IGMP

    Unicast goes to one device. Broadcast goes to every device on the subnet. Multicast goes to a group, and devices join the groups they want. Dante multicast flows, AES67 streams and sACN lighting all use it.

    • Without help, a switch treats multicast like broadcast and floods it to every port. A few streams are fine; lots will swamp slow devices and Wi-Fi.
    • IGMP snooping makes the switch listen for "I want this group" messages and send each stream only to ports that asked for it.
    • Snooping needs one IGMP querier per network or VLAN, usually the core switch, to keep the group list up to date.

    Basics

    How Dante thinks

    • Devices are anything with a Dante port: stageboxes, desk cards, amps, processors, wireless receivers, a laptop running Dante Virtual Soundcard.
    • Each device has transmit channels (audio it puts on the network) and receive channels (audio it can take in).
    • A subscription connects one transmit channel to one receive channel. In Dante Controller it's a tick in the routing grid: the receiver "subscribes" to the transmitter.
    • Subscriptions are stored by device name and channel name, not by cable or port. Rename a device and every subscription to it breaks; plug it into a different switch port and nothing changes.
    • Routing lives in the devices themselves. Dante Controller is only the remote control: close it and the audio keeps flowing.

    Basics

    Flows: unicast and multicast

    Audio doesn't travel channel by channel. Dante groups channels into flows. A unicast flow goes from one transmitter to one receiver and carries up to 4 channels. A multicast flow is sent once and any number of receivers can pick it up; it carries up to 8 channels by default. Every device can only handle a limited number of flows. Typical hardware supports around 32 each way, and small adapters as few as two. That limit, not channel count, is often what you hit first.

    • Unicast is the default and is best when one receiver wants a channel.
    • When several receivers want the same channels (FOH, monitors, recorder, broadcast), each unicast receiver costs the transmitter more flows. Switching those channels to multicast uses one set of flows for everyone.
    • Multicast reaches every switch port unless your switches use IGMP snooping to send it only where it's wanted. Use it where it helps, not everywhere.
    • If a receiver runs out of flows (single channels from many different devices), multicast won't help. Change the routing instead.

    Timing

    Clocking

    Every Dante device must sample audio at exactly the same moment, or you get clicks and dropouts. They stay in step using PTP (Precision Time Protocol): one device is elected leader clock and every other device follows it, kept within about a microsecond.

    • The leader is chosen automatically from each device's clock quality and link speed. If it disappears, another takes over within seconds.
    • Preferred leader: tick it on the device you want in charge (usually the main desk or a device with a stable word clock). With several ticked, the lowest MAC address wins.
    • Sync to external: lets a device follow a house word clock or its host console, then pass that timing on to the network.
    • All devices that exchange audio must use the same sample rate. A mismatched device can't subscribe.

    Clock warning signs

    • Two or more leaders in Dante Controller: usually a network loop, or clock packets leaking between VLANs that should be separate.
    • "Clock instability" or ticking: often Energy Efficient Ethernet (EEE) on a switch, an overloaded 100 Mb link, or a switch without proper QoS.
    • A device stuck "unlocked": check its sample rate and whether it's set to sync to an external clock that isn't there.
    Timing

    Latency: how much buffer to allow

    Each receiver waits a fixed time before playing audio, so packets that took longer through the network still arrive in time. Lower latency is quicker but leaves less margin. The usual default of 1 ms works on large networks. Small all-gigabit systems can go lower. Two rules are easy to forget: a device on a 100 Mb link needs at least 1 ms or its subscriptions fail, and receivers using the same latency from the same transmitter stay sample-aligned.

    Redundancy: primary and secondary

    Many Dante devices have two ports. With redundancy, the device sends every packet on both its primary and its secondary port at the same time, onto two completely separate networks. If a cable, switch or power supply fails on one, the audio carries on over the other with no gap.

    • Primary and secondary must be two separate networks: separate switches (best) or separate VLANs. Never link them together.
    • The secondary port is not for daisy-chaining to the next device.
    • Both ports must run at the same speed.
    • Run Dante Controller on the primary network.
    • Devices with only one port join the primary network only.
    Network

    Addresses and names

    • Out of the box, Dante devices find an address on their own. With a DHCP server on the network they take one from it. Without one they pick a link-local address: 169.254.x.x on primary and 172.31.x.x on secondary.
    • Static addresses are only worth it when the design needs them (a shared network, control systems that address devices directly). Keep a record, and keep every device on the same subnet.
    • A device given a static address on a different subnet appears in Dante Controller but can't route audio with the others.
    • Device names must be unique. Name them by job and position: SB-STAGE-L, FOH-DESK, AMP-RACK-1.
    • Name channels on the transmitter ("Kick", "Vox 1"): those labels follow the audio into every receiver.
    • Replacing a broken box? Give the new one the same name and the old subscriptions come back by themselves.
    • Save a preset of the whole system before the show so you can recall it after a swap or a reset.

    • Energy Efficient Ethernet (EEE, "green Ethernet") off on every port carrying Dante. It is the classic cause of clock problems.
    • Gigabit between switches.
    • IGMP snooping with a querier if you use multicast, so it doesn't flood every port. If problems start after enabling it, test with it off.
    • No Wi-Fi in the audio path.
    • A dedicated network, or a VLAN of its own, for large channel counts. If you use VLANs, keep Dante's clock from leaking between them.
    Beyond Dante

    AES67, ST 2110 and other brands

    AES67 is an open standard for audio over IP that many brands support (Ravenna, Livewire+, Q-SYS and others). Dante devices that support it can send and receive AES67 streams alongside normal Dante audio. SMPTE ST 2110-30 is the broadcast version of the same idea.

    • Turn on AES67 mode for the device in Dante Controller, then create a multicast AES67 flow. These streams are always multicast and use addresses in the 239.x.x.x range.
    • Other brands find the stream from its announcement (SAP/SDP). Some need it entered by hand.
    • Dante devices talk to each other with normal Dante flows, not AES67.

    Not the same thing

    • AVB / Milan is a different audio network family (used by some speaker and console brands). It needs AVB-capable switches and doesn't pass audio to Dante directly. A bridge or a device with both is needed.
    • MADI is point-to-point digital audio on coax or fibre, not a network: one cable, up to 64 channels, no switches.
    • Lighting (Art-Net, sACN) and control (OSC) can share switches with Dante if QoS is right, but many shows keep them on separate networks or VLANs.
